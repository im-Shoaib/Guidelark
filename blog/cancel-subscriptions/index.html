How to Cancel Subscriptions You Don't Use

How to find every subscription you pay for, cancel the ones you don't use, avoid cancellation traps, and work out what you save in a year.

The short version

  • Most people underestimate what they spend on subscriptions. A 30-minute audit of your statements finds them all.
  • Ask three questions for each one, then cancel, downgrade or keep it on purpose.
  • In the example, cancelling four services and downgrading one saves $1,235.52 a year.

Somewhere in your bank statement, a service you forgot about is taking money out of your account every month. Maybe you signed up for a free trial in January. Maybe you meant to cancel after one month. Either way, it is still there, quietly charging you.

This guide shows you how to cancel subscriptions you don't use, find every one you are paying for, and stop the same thing from happening again. You will do a full audit in about 30 minutes, cancel what is not worth keeping, and work out exactly how much you will save over a year. No app to buy. Just your bank statement, your phone, and a list.

What Is a Subscription Audit?

A subscription audit is a once-over of every recurring payment leaving your account, so you can decide which ones still earn their place. You list each service, what it costs, when you last used it, and whether to keep, cancel, or downgrade it. One list, honest answers, and a few cancellations.

Why Subscriptions Pile Up So Fast

Subscriptions are built to be forgotten. You sign up in two minutes, the payment is small, and auto-renew does the rest.

A 2025 CNET survey conducted with YouGov, covering 2,440 US adults, found that households spend close to $1,100 a year on subscriptions, and roughly $200 of that goes toward services they rarely or never use (CNET and YouGov survey, 2025). In the same survey, 61 percent of people said they were reconsidering at least one subscription because of money worries.

An earlier study shows why the total surprises people. C+R Research asked 1,000 consumers to guess their monthly subscription spending, then checked the real numbers. On average, people guessed $86 a month. They actually spent $219. In that study, 74 percent said recurring charges were easy to forget, and 42 percent said they had kept paying for a service after they stopped using it (C+R Research, 2022, figures reported in the same release).

Step 1: Find Every Subscription You Pay For

Start with your money, not your memory.

Open the last two or three months of bank and card statements and look for every repeating charge. Small ones count.

Then check the places statements miss:

  • iPhone: Settings, tap your name at the top, then Subscriptions. You will see every active subscription billed through Apple.
  • Android: Open Google Play, tap your profile icon, then Payments and subscriptions, then Subscriptions.
  • Email: Search for "receipt," "renewal," "trial," and "your subscription."
  • PayPal: Check Settings, then Payments, then Manage automatic payments.
  • Amazon: Go to Account, then Memberships and Subscriptions.
  • Annual charges: Check a full year back if you can. Antivirus, cloud storage, and password managers often bill once a year.

Write down every single one, even the ones you plan to keep.

Step 2: Run the Audit With a Simple Table

Put your list into a table with four columns. You can copy this one into a notes app or a spreadsheet.

Service Cost per month Last used Decision
Your streaming service $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade
Your music app $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade
Your fitness app $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade
Your cloud storage $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade
Your free trial $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade
Your annual service $ Keep / Cancel / Downgrade

For the "Last used" column, be honest. "I think last month" is not an answer. Check the app if you are not sure.

If a service bills once a year, divide the yearly price by 12. A $60 annual charge is $5 a month.

Step 3: Decide What Stays and What Goes

Go through the table one row at a time and ask three questions:

  1. Did I use it in the last 30 days?
  2. Would I miss it this week if it disappeared?
  3. Is there a free or cheaper version that does the same job?

If the answer to the first two is no, cancel it. Do not keep a subscription for the version of yourself who might use it someday. You can always sign up again later, and most services let you rejoin in under a minute.

A few calls are less clear cut:

  • Duplicates. Two music apps, two streaming services with the same shows, two cloud storage plans. Keep the one you use most and cancel the other.
  • The "maybe" pile. If you feel torn, cancel anyway and set a phone reminder for two weeks from now. If you never thought about it, you made the right call.
  • Shared accounts. If family or roommates use it, ask them before you cancel. One quick message avoids a pointless argument.
  • Annual plans. Check the renewal date first. If it renews next week, cancel now. If it renews in nine months, put the date in your calendar.

Downgrades count as wins too. If you love a service but only use the basic features, the cheaper tier is the right call. A family plan with one person on it is a downgrade waiting to happen.

Step 4: Cancel Them the Right Way

Here is the rule that saves the most frustration: cancel where you signed up. Apple subscriptions get cancelled in Apple, Google Play ones in Google Play, website sign-ups on the website. Trying to cancel in the wrong place is the most common reason people think a cancellation "didn't work."

On iPhone: Settings, tap your name, Subscriptions, pick the service, tap Cancel Subscription, confirm. Your access usually runs until the end of the billing period.

On Android: Google Play, profile icon, Payments and subscriptions, Subscriptions, pick the service, Cancel subscription, follow the prompts.

On a website: Log in on a computer, not just the app. Look under Account, Billing, Membership, or Manage Plan. If you cannot find it, search the web for the service name plus "how to cancel."

Expect some friction. Companies know every extra screen loses them a few cancellations, so they use what people call dark patterns:

  • Retention offers. A screen that says "Wait, how about 50% off?" That is a new deal, not a cancellation. Keep clicking past it.
  • The pause trap. "Pause for a month instead?" A pause restarts on its own. Only pick it if you genuinely want to come back.
  • Chat or phone only. Some companies make you talk to someone to cancel. Stay polite, repeat "I would like to cancel, please," and do not accept an offer you did not ask for.
  • The hidden button. "Cancel" buried under "Manage" or a tiny "Continue to cancel" link. Slow down and read every button.

When it goes through, screenshot the confirmation page and save the confirmation email. Check your next statement to make sure the charge is gone.

Two more things. Deleting an app does not cancel its subscription. Cancel first, delete second. And for most free trials, you can cancel the day you sign up and still use the trial until it ends.

A Worked Example: What Cancelling Actually Saves

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. It shows how the math adds up when you cancel four services and downgrade one.

Service Cost per month Action
Streaming service B (never watched) $15.99 Cancel
Second music app (duplicate) $10.99 Cancel
Fitness app (last used in February) $12.99 Cancel
Meal kit box (never unpaused) $49.99 Cancel
Cancelled total $89.96

Now the downgrade. A premium streaming plan costs $22.99 a month. The basic plan costs $9.99 a month and has everything this household watches. That is $22.99 minus $9.99, which saves $13.00 a month.

Add it up:

  • Cancelled subscriptions: $89.96 per month times 12 months equals $1,079.52 per year.
  • Downgrade saving: $13.00 per month times 12 months equals $156.00 per year.
  • Total yearly saving: $1,079.52 plus $156.00 equals $1,235.52.

That is real money. Send it somewhere on purpose instead of letting it drift. Sinking funds for beginners shows a simple way to park monthly savings for costs you know are coming, and how to budget on less than $1,000 a month shows how to fit the freed-up money into your plan.

Step 5: Stop It From Piling Up Again

Cancelling once helps. A few habits keep the list short:

  • Trial rule. When you start a free trial, set a phone reminder for two days before it ends, on the spot.
  • One card for subscriptions. Put all recurring payments on one card or account. When you review that one statement, you see everything.
  • Quarterly check. Every three months, spend ten minutes repeating the audit.
  • Annual renewal list. Keep a short list of yearly subscriptions and their renewal dates. Check it at the start of each month.
  • The sign-up test. Before starting a new subscription, ask: "Would I sign up for this again next month at full price?" If the answer is no, skip the trial too. The how to stop impulse spending guide has more on slowing down that kind of spending.

What to Do When a Company Will Not Let You Cancel

Most cancellations go through fine. If a company hides the cancel button, refuses your request, or keeps charging you after you cancelled, do not just give up.

First, keep a record. Screenshot the cancellation page, save the confirmation email, and note the date and time of any call.

Second, try again in writing. Email or message support with a short, clear request: the service name, the account email, the date you first tried to cancel, and a request for written confirmation. Written requests are harder to ignore than phone calls.

Third, if the charge keeps coming, contact your bank or card issuer and dispute it. Show them you cancelled. You can also complain to your state attorney general or the FTC in the US, or to the consumer protection body where you live.

In the US, the FTC's 2024 "click to cancel" rule, which would have required companies to make cancelling as easy as signing up, was vacated by the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals in July 2025. The FTC issued a new advance notice of proposed rulemaking in March 2026, but no replacement federal rule is in force (FTC advance notice of proposed rulemaking, March 2026). Other protections still apply, including the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act and roughly 30 state automatic-renewal laws.

In the UK, a new subscription contracts regime under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 is being implemented. It will require traders to give clear information before sign-up, send reminders at key points, and provide a straightforward way to exit, including online exit when you signed up online. It also includes a 14-day cooling-off period after a trial or a long-term contract auto-renews. The UK government estimates the regime will deliver about 400 million pounds a year in consumer benefit, working out to about 14 pounds a month saved for each unwanted subscription a consumer can leave earlier (UK government response to the subscription contracts consultation).

Rules differ by country, and they change. Check the rules where you live before you assume you have no options.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find all my subscriptions?

Check two or three months of bank and card statements, then look in Apple Subscriptions, Google Play Subscriptions, PayPal automatic payments, and Amazon Memberships and Subscriptions. Search your email for "receipt" and "renewal" to catch the rest.

Will I lose access right away if I cancel?

Usually not. Most services keep your access until the end of the billing period you already paid for. The confirmation screen normally shows the date your access ends.

Can I get a refund for subscriptions I forgot to cancel?

For past months, usually not, but it costs nothing to ask. If a company charged you after you cancelled, dispute the charge with your bank and show your cancellation proof.

What happens if I just delete the app?

Nothing changes with your billing. Deleting an app does not cancel its subscription. Cancel the subscription first, in the right place, and then delete the app.

How often should I audit my subscriptions?

Every three months is a good rhythm, plus a quick check before any annual renewal.

Can I pause a subscription instead of cancelling it?

Many services offer a pause, and it can make sense for something seasonal. Set a reminder for the restart date, because a paused subscription starts charging again on its own.

Your Next Step

Open your bank statement and list every subscription today. Then cancel one. Just one. The first cancellation is the hardest part. The money you save is yours to use on purpose, not theirs to keep by accident.
