How to Read Your Paycheck in the US (2026 Guide)

How to read your paycheck: gross pay, FICA, federal and state tax, pre-tax and post-tax deductions and YTD, with a worked example and a 7-step check.

The short version

  • Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) together should equal 7.65% of gross pay below the wage base.

Your first paycheck can feel like a trick. The job offer said one number, and the bank deposit shows a smaller one. Learning how to read your paycheck clears that up in about ten minutes. This guide walks through every section of a US pay stub, explains each tax and deduction in plain language, and shows you a worked example with real math. By the end, you will know exactly where your money goes before it reaches you, and how to spot a mistake.

What Is a Pay Stub?

A pay stub, also called a pay statement or earnings statement, is the breakdown that comes with your paycheck. It lists what you earned in the pay period, what was taken out for taxes and benefits, and what is left as net pay. Your employer may hand you a paper stub, post one in an online payroll portal, or send it by email.

Federal law does not require your employer to give you a pay stub, but most employers do, and some states have their own rules. If you never see one, ask payroll or HR where to find it. (U.S. Department of Labor, Questions and Answers About the FLSA)

The Parts of a Paycheck, Top to Bottom

Read your stub top to bottom, not bottom to top. The top tells you whether the paycheck is even yours and which work it covers.

1. Who, When, and How Much Time

The top of the stub lists your name, your employer's name, the pay period dates, and the pay date. If you are paid hourly, it also shows the hours you worked and your pay rate.

Check these lines first. A wrong pay period can throw off everything below it. Compare the hours to your timecard or schedule.

2. Earnings: Gross Pay

Gross pay is everything you earned in the pay period before anything comes out. That can include regular pay, overtime, bonuses, commissions, tips, and holiday pay. It is the number your salary offer talked about. It is not the number you get to spend.

If you are hourly, multiply your rate by your hours. At $22 an hour for 80 hours, gross pay is $1,760. If you are salaried, divide your annual salary by the number of pay periods. A $52,000 salary paid every two weeks is $2,000 gross per check.

Overtime follows its own rule. Federal law requires at least time and a half for hours over 40 in a week for covered nonexempt workers. So 5 overtime hours at a $20 base rate pay $150. If your hours change from week to week, your gross pay changes too, and that is why budgeting from one steady number can fail. Read next: How to Budget With Irregular Income.

Taxes: The Biggest Chunk

Taxes are usually the biggest reason your deposit is smaller than your gross pay. Most US pay stubs show three kinds: federal income tax, FICA taxes, and state or local income tax. Each one works differently, so take them one at a time.

What Does FICA Mean on My Paycheck?

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It is two taxes bundled under one name: Social Security and Medicare. You pay one share, and your employer pays a matching share. Your stub may show them as two lines, or as one line labeled FICA.

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your wages, but only up to a yearly cap called the wage base. For 2026, that cap is $184,500 (Social Security Administration, Contribution and Benefit Base). Once your year-to-date wages pass that amount, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the year. At the cap, the most an employee pays in 2026 is $11,439.

Medicare tax is 1.45% of all covered wages, with no cap. If your wages from one employer pass $200,000 in the year, your employer also withholds an extra 0.9% Medicare tax on the amount above that line. Your employer does not match that extra part. (IRS, Publication 15 (2026)))

Here is a fast way to check the FICA lines. Below the wage base, Social Security plus Medicare should equal 7.65% of your gross pay. Multiply your gross pay by 0.0765 and compare. If the two lines add up to that number, FICA is doing what it should.

Federal Income Tax

Federal income tax is the line most people find hardest to predict, because it is not a flat percentage. Your employer figures it from your pay, your pay frequency, and the Form W-4 you filled out when you started the job.

Form W-4, the Employee's Withholding Certificate, tells your employer your filing status, your dependents, and any extra adjustments. Step 1 covers personal information and filing status. Step 2 covers multiple jobs or a working spouse. Step 3 covers dependents. Step 4 covers other income, deductions, or an extra amount you want withheld. Step 5 is your signature.

Your W-4 does not change your total tax bill. It changes when you pay it. Withhold too much and you get a refund, which means you lent the IRS money for free all year. Withhold too little and you owe money when you file. If your life changes, update the form: a new job, a marriage, a second job, or a new dependent.

State and Local Income Tax

Most states also take income tax from your paycheck, and the amount depends on where you live and work. A few states have no income tax at all. Some cities charge a local income tax on top. If you moved to a new state mid-year, you may see two state lines, one for each state. State rules vary a lot, so check your own state tax agency if a state line looks wrong.

Other Deductions: Pre-Tax vs Post-Tax

After taxes come the benefit and savings deductions. The key split is pre-tax versus post-tax. Pre-tax deductions come out before income tax is figured, so they lower the wages your tax is based on. Post-tax deductions come out after tax, so they do not change your taxable wages.

Common pre-tax deductions include a traditional 401(k), many health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, and some commuter benefits. Common post-tax deductions include a Roth 401(k), some life insurance premiums, union dues, loan repayments, charitable giving through payroll, and wage garnishments.

Your stub may use short codes for these, and the codes vary by employer. If a deduction surprises you, check what you signed up for during onboarding. A plan you forgot about, like dental coverage you never use, is the most common surprise.

One line deserves a second look: your 401(k). A traditional 401(k) is pre-tax, so it lowers your taxable pay now and you pay tax when you withdraw in retirement. A Roth 401(k) is post-tax, so you pay tax now and withdraw tax-free later if the rules are met.

What Does YTD Mean on a Pay Stub?

YTD stands for year-to-date. It is the running total of each line from January 1 through the current paycheck: YTD gross pay, YTD federal tax, YTD Social Security, and so on.

YTD numbers are useful in three ways. First, they show whether an error started earlier, because a wrong YTD means the problem is not new. Second, they show how close you are to the Social Security wage base. Third, your W-2 at year end should roughly match the final YTD figures.

A Worked Example: From Gross Pay to Net Pay

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. It follows one biweekly paycheck for an hourly worker earning $22 an hour for 80 hours.

Pay stub line This paycheck What it means
Gross pay $1,760.00 $22 x 80 hours
Federal income tax $132.00 Withholding per W-4 (illustration)
Social Security (6.2%) $109.12 $1,760 x 6.2%
Medicare (1.45%) $25.52 $1,760 x 1.45%
State income tax $52.00 Varies by state (illustration)
Health insurance $85.00 Your share of the premium
401(k) (4%) $70.40 $1,760 x 4%
Total deductions $474.04 All lines added together
Net pay $1,285.96 $1,760.00 - $474.04

Here is the math, step by step. Step 1, gross pay: $22 times 80 hours equals $1,760. Step 2, FICA: $109.12 plus $25.52 equals $134.64, and $1,760 times 7.65% equals $134.64, so the two FICA lines check out. Step 3, all deductions: $132.00 plus $109.12 plus $25.52 plus $52.00 plus $85.00 plus $70.40 equals $474.04. Step 4, net pay: $1,760.00 minus $474.04 equals $1,285.96. That $1,285.96 is the deposit you would see in your bank account.

One honest note about this example: in real life, pre-tax deductions such as a 401(k) and many health premiums lower the wages your taxes are figured on, so the tax lines would be a little smaller.

If this is your fifth paycheck of the year and every check looked the same, YTD gross pay would be 5 times $1,760, which is $8,800. That is how the YTD column grows: one paycheck at a time.

Why Is My Paycheck Less Than My Salary?

Because your salary is gross pay. Taxes, insurance, and retirement contributions come out before the money reaches you. A $52,000 salary paid every two weeks is $2,000 gross per check, and net pay is always lower.

Other things can shrink a check too. A partial first pay period, unpaid time off, a new benefit election, or a W-4 change can all move the deposit. How Do I Check If My Paycheck Is Correct?

Run this quick check every payday. It takes about five minutes once you know the lines.

  1. Hours and rate match your timecard or schedule.
  2. Gross pay math works: rate times hours, plus overtime at time and a half if any.
  3. FICA checks out: Social Security equals gross pay times 6.2%, Medicare equals gross pay times 1.45%, as long as you are below the yearly wage base.
  4. Federal withholding looks close to your recent checks. A big jump means something changed, such as a bonus, a W-4 update, or extra pay.
  5. Benefit deductions match what you signed up for, with no plan you do not recognize.
  6. YTD figures grew by about one paycheck since last time.
  7. Net pay matches the deposit in your bank account.

What to Do If Something Looks Wrong

Talk to payroll or HR first, and soon, with the stub in front of you. Name the pay period and the exact line that looks off. Most errors are simple: wrong hours, a missed overtime rate, or a duplicate deduction.

Keep copies of your stubs. Download them from the payroll portal or save the paper copies. They matter for tax filing, loan applications, and proof of income.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?

Gross pay is everything you earned before anything comes out. Net pay is what is left after taxes and deductions, and it is the amount that lands in your bank account. Your salary offer is a gross pay number.

What does FICA mean on my paycheck?

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It covers Social Security tax at 6.2% of wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, and Medicare tax at 1.45% of all covered wages.

What does YTD mean on a pay stub?

YTD means year-to-date. It shows the running total of each line from January 1 through the current paycheck.

Why is my first paycheck smaller than I expected?

First checks often cover a partial pay period, so the gross pay is lower than a full period. Benefit deductions may also start right away. Check the pay period dates and hours first before you assume something is wrong.

Do I need to keep my pay stubs?

Yes, at least until your W-2 arrives and you have filed your taxes. Digital copies from your payroll portal work fine.

What if my employer does not give me a pay stub?

Federal law does not require your employer to hand you one, but most employers provide one, and some states have their own rules. Ask payroll or HR where to find yours.
