Sinking Funds for Beginners: Save a Little Each Month

Learn how sinking funds for beginners work. Turn big irregular bills into small monthly savings with a simple starter plan.

The short version

  • A sinking fund is money you save each month for a cost you know is coming, like insurance or holidays.
  • The formula: what you still need, divided by the months left, is your monthly amount.
  • Start with one to three funds, keep them apart from your emergency fund, and check them once a month.

Some bills show up every month, like rent and groceries. Others show up once or twice a year and hit much harder: car insurance, holiday gifts, a car service, a phone that needs replacing. If you pay those from a single paycheck, one month can wipe out your budget.

A sinking fund fixes that by spreading the cost. You save a small amount each month for each expense you know is coming. When the bill arrives, the money is already there.

What Is a Sinking Fund?

A sinking fund is money you set aside monthly for an expense you know is coming. You pick the expense, estimate the cost, and divide it by the months you have to save. When the bill arrives, you pay it from the fund instead of from one paycheck. The fund is refilled for the next time the expense comes around.

Here is a simple example. Your car insurance costs $900 a year. Divide $900 by 12 months and you get $75 a month. Put $75 aside each month, and when the renewal arrives, the full $900 is waiting. Nothing about your normal monthly budget has to change that month.

Sinking funds work for fun costs too, not just bills. A trip, holiday gifts, or a new laptop are all fair game. If you know the cost is coming and you can estimate the amount, it belongs in a sinking fund.

Sinking Fund vs Emergency Fund

This is the distinction that matters most, so let us get it straight early. A sinking fund pays for costs you can predict. An emergency fund pays for costs you cannot predict. They are two different jobs, and one pot of money cannot do both well.

If holiday gifts or car insurance drain your emergency fund every year, your safety net is gone before a real emergency ever shows up. Keep the two separate, and each one can do its job.

Sinking fund Emergency fund
Pays for Costs you can predict Costs you cannot predict
Examples Annual insurance, holiday gifts, car service Job loss, urgent medical bill, sudden major repair
Target amount The cost of the expense Often 3 to 6 months of essential costs
When you use it When the planned expense arrives Only for a true emergency
After you use it Refill for the next round Rebuild as soon as you can

One more difference is worth knowing. A sinking fund is meant to be spent. When you use it for its planned purpose, the plan worked. An emergency fund, by contrast, should sit untouched until something genuinely unexpected happens. If you have not built an emergency fund yet, read how to build an emergency fund on a small income first, then come back and set up sinking funds alongside it.

Why Sinking Funds Make Budgeting Easier

A monthly budget handles monthly bills well. It struggles with costs that do not follow a monthly schedule. A $900 insurance bill, a $600 car service, or a holiday season that costs hundreds of dollars can each knock a good budget off track in a single month.

Sinking funds turn those costs into ordinary monthly line items. The total you pay does not change. The timing does. Instead of finding $900 in one month, you find $75 in twelve months.

The numbers show why this matters. In the Federal Reserve's latest survey of US households, 63 percent of adults said they would cover an unexpected $400 expense using cash or its equivalent, which means more than a third would not. When a predictable bill is paid from the same thin cushion, there is even less left for the real surprises.

Holiday spending is a good example of a predictable cost that still catches people out. In the National Retail Federation's 2025 holiday survey, US consumers planned to spend an average of $890.49 per person on holiday gifts, food, decorations, and other seasonal items, including $627.93 on gifts for family and friends.

How to Start a Sinking Fund

You do not need special software or a complicated system. Five steps will get your first funds running.

Step 1: List your irregular expenses

Look back over the last 12 months of bank statements and list every cost that does not show up every month. Annual and semiannual bills are the obvious ones: insurance, car registration, subscriptions that renew once a year. Then add seasonal spending, like holidays and birthdays, and maintenance, like a car service or new tires.

Step 2: Price each expense

Use last year's actual cost where you can. Check renewal notices, old receipts, or past bank statements. If you do not have a past cost, get a real quote. Add a small buffer, around 5 to 10 percent, for price rises.

Step 3: Divide the cost by the months you have left

Take each target amount and divide it by the number of months until you need the money. A $600 expense due in 12 months needs $50 a month. The same expense due in 6 months needs $100 a month. If the monthly amount is too high, you have three options: lower the target, move the date, or start earlier next time.

Step 4: Add the monthly amounts to your budget

Treat each sinking fund like a bill. Put it in your budget as a line item and move the money on payday, not at the end of the month when little is left. If you use a percentage-based budget, sinking funds sit inside the savings and needs portions. For a refresher on splitting your income, see the 50/30/20 rule explained.

Step 5: Automate and review once a month

Set up an automatic transfer on payday if your bank allows it. Once a month, check each fund against its target. If a bill goes up, adjust the monthly amount. If a fund is ahead, leave it alone. A five-minute monthly check keeps the whole system honest.

How Much Should You Put in a Sinking Fund Each Month?

Use this formula for every fund:

(Target amount minus already saved) divided by months left = monthly amount

If you need $900 in 12 months and have $0 saved, the math is ($900 minus $0) divided by 12, which is $75 a month. If you already have $180 saved and 9 months left, it is ($900 minus $180) divided by 9, which is $80 a month.

Below is a starter set of sinking funds with made-up numbers to show how the math works. This is an illustration with made-up numbers. Replace every figure with your own costs.

Expense Yearly cost Monthly amount
Car maintenance and repairs $600 $50
Car insurance $900 $75
Holiday gifts and food $600 $50
Annual subscriptions and renewals $120 $10
Phone replacement $240 $20
Travel and trips $480 $40
Total $2,940 $245

Check the totals: $600 plus $900 plus $600 plus $120 plus $240 plus $480 equals $2,940 a year. Divide $2,940 by 12 and you get $245 a month. Every row follows the same rule, yearly cost divided by 12 equals monthly amount.

If $245 a month does not fit your budget, start with two or three funds, the ones tied to the bills that cause the most stress.

A Worked Example

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. Say it is January, and you set up three funds:

  • Car insurance: $900 due in 12 months. $900 divided by 12 is $75 a month.
  • Holiday spending: $600 due in 11 months, in December. $600 divided by 11 is $54.55, so you round up to $55 a month. After 11 months that gives $605, which leaves a $5 cushion.
  • Car maintenance: $600 spread over 12 months. $600 divided by 12 is $50 a month.

Total monthly saving: $75 plus $55 plus $50 equals $180 a month.

After 6 months, the balances are: insurance $450 (6 times $75), holidays $330 (6 times $55), maintenance $300 (6 times $50). Added together, that is $1,080 set aside. Check: $450 plus $330 plus $300 equals $1,080.

In December, the holiday fund holds $605 and covers the spending. The insurance fund holds $900 when renewal arrives. The maintenance fund covers the car service. None of those months requires finding a lump sum from one paycheck. After each fund is used, you start refilling it for the next round.

Where Should You Keep Your Sinking Funds?

Keep sinking funds separate from your everyday checking account. If the money sits in the same account you spend from, it will get spent. Separation is the whole point.

Most people use one of these setups:

  • Separate savings account. One account for all sinking funds, tracked with a simple spreadsheet. This is the easiest option.
  • Sub-accounts or buckets. Some banks and apps let you split one savings account into labeled buckets, one per fund.
  • Spreadsheet or budgeting app. If your bank does not offer buckets, a spreadsheet with one row per fund works fine. Our free sinking funds spreadsheet does the monthly math for you, or see our comparison of free budgeting apps.

Keep the money easy to reach. Sinking funds pay bills that arrive on a schedule, so the money should not be locked away or invested in anything that can drop in value. A plain savings account is the usual choice.

Sinking Funds on a Tight Budget

Sinking funds help most when money is tight, because a surprise $500 bill hurts most when there is no slack in the budget. But the system has to fit what you actually earn.

Start with one fund, the bill that causes the most stress. If that is car insurance, start there. If it is holiday spending, start there. One fund that works beats five funds that collapse.

Small amounts still count. Even $10 to $25 a month builds a real cushion over a year. $25 a month for 12 months is $300, which covers a car service or a modest holiday budget.

Look for money already leaving your budget. Pausing one subscription for a few months can fund a sinking fund. Cutting grocery waste can do the same. For practical ideas, see 25 ways to save money on groceries without coupons.

Windfalls can jump-start a fund. A tax refund, a work bonus, or cash from selling something you no longer need can cover the first few months at once. After that, the regular monthly amount keeps it going.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Raiding a fund for something else. A sinking fund only works if each fund pays for its named purpose. Borrowing from the car fund to pay for a weekend away leaves you short when the car bill arrives.

Starting with too many funds. Three to five funds is a sensible start. Ten funds on day one turns a simple system into admin work, and most people quit.

Guessing costs instead of checking. A fund built on a guess is often too small. Check last year's spending, renewal notices, or real quotes.

Mixing sinking funds with the emergency fund. Keep them separate, in different accounts or buckets if you can. A planned bill is not an emergency.

Setting the target and forgetting it. Prices change, plans change, and cars get older. Review each fund once a month and adjust the monthly amount when the target moves.

FAQ

How much should I put in a sinking fund each month?

Take the target amount, subtract what you have already saved, and divide by the months left. A $900 bill due in 12 months with nothing saved needs $75 a month.

What is the difference between a sinking fund and an emergency fund?

A sinking fund pays for costs you can predict, like insurance, holidays, or a car service. An emergency fund pays for costs you cannot predict, like a job loss or an urgent medical bill. You need both, kept separate.

Where should I keep a sinking fund?

In a savings account that is separate from your everyday checking, ideally with labeled sub-accounts or buckets for each fund. Keep it easy to reach, since the money is meant to be spent on a schedule.

How many sinking funds should I have?

Start with three to five, covering the irregular costs that stress you most. Common starters are car costs, holidays, and one annual bill.

Can I use sinking funds if I live paycheck to paycheck?

Yes. Start with one fund and a small amount, even $10 to $25 a month. Small, steady deposits still beat putting a predictable bill on a credit card.

Start With One Fund

Pick the irregular bill that causes you the most stress. Price it, divide it by the months you have left, and set up one fund this week. If you'd rather not do the math by hand, the free sinking funds spreadsheet works it out for every fund at once. Move the first deposit on your next payday, and set a monthly reminder to check the balance.
