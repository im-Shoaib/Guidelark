How to Avoid Bank Fees: 8 Fixes That Save You Money

How to avoid bank fees: monthly, overdraft, NSF, ATM and other charges, the fix for each one, and how to ask your bank for a refund.

The short version

  • Check your last three statements and your fee schedule to see which fees you actually pay.
  • Meet waiver rules on purpose, check your overdraft opt-in, and use in-network ATMs.
  • If a fee still hits, ask for a refund. If it keeps happening, switch to a no-fee account.

Bank fees are small charges that add up fast. A monthly maintenance fee here, an ATM surcharge there, one overdraft on a bad week, and suddenly a few hundred dollars a year has left your account for nothing you wanted. This guide shows you how to avoid bank fees with eight fixes, one for each fee that hits beginners most.

What is a bank fee?

A bank fee is a charge your bank or credit union takes from your account for a service, a shortfall, or for not meeting an account rule. Common examples are monthly maintenance fees, overdraft fees, ATM fees, and paper statement fees. Fees vary by bank and by account, so your own fee schedule is the final word on what you pay.

Step 1: Find out what you are actually paying

Before you fix anything, look at your last three statements and circle every fee. Write down the fee name, the amount, and the date. Most people find one or two repeat offenders.

Next, find your account's fee schedule. It is usually in your banking app under documents, or on the bank's website. Read the waiver rules for each fee on your list. A waiver rule is the condition that makes the fee disappear, such as a direct deposit or a minimum balance. Once you know the rules, the fixes below get simple. 1. Monthly maintenance fees

A monthly maintenance fee, also called a monthly service fee, is a charge for keeping the account open. Many accounts waive it if you meet one condition, usually a monthly direct deposit, a minimum balance, or e-statements. The fix is to meet the waiver rule on purpose, every month, instead of hoping it happens.

Start by asking which rule applies to your account, then set it up. If the waiver needs a direct deposit, ask your employer or school to split part of your pay into this account. If it needs a minimum balance, treat that balance as a floor you do not cross. If you cannot meet the rule reliably, a no-monthly-fee account is usually the cheaper choice. According to Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study, 47 percent of non-interest checking accounts charge no monthly fee at all, and another 48 percent waive the fee with direct deposit.

2. Overdraft fees

An overdraft fee is charged when your bank covers a transaction even though your available balance is too low. The average overdraft fee was $26.77 in 2025, according to Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study, and 94 percent of accounts surveyed still charge it.

Here is the fix that matters most in the US. Under Regulation E, your bank cannot charge you overdraft fees on ATM and one-time debit card transactions unless you affirmatively opted in to that coverage. If you never opted in, or you are not sure, check your overdraft settings in your app or ask your bank. You can revoke your opt-in at any time. With no opt-in, a debit purchase that would overdraw your account is usually declined instead of covered for a fee. That rule does not cover checks, recurring debit payments, or ACH payments, so those can still overdraw you.

Three more habits keep overdrafts away. First, keep a small buffer in checking, even $50 to $100, and treat it as zero. Second, set a low-balance alert so your phone tells you before a payment lands. Third, link a savings account as backup if your bank offers free overdraft transfers. If your budget is tight, how to budget on less than $1,000 a month shows how to build that buffer into your plan.

One thing to know about the rules: in 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) finalized a rule that would have capped overdraft fees at $5 for large banks, but Congress overturned it in May 2025. There is no federal cap now, so your bank's own fee schedule decides what you pay.

If you bank in the UK, the rules are different. Since April 2020, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) has required banks to charge overdrafts as a single simple annual interest rate, with no fixed daily or monthly fees, and unarranged overdrafts cannot cost more than arranged ones. Check your bank's overdraft interest rate and any arranged limit before you borrow.

3. NSF fees

An NSF fee, or nonsufficient funds fee, is charged when your bank returns a payment unpaid because your balance is too low. The average NSF fee fell to a record low of $16.82 in 2025, according to Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study, but it is still money lost for nothing.

The fix is the same family as overdrafts: a buffer, low-balance alerts, and tracking pending payments. Pending payments are the tricky part. A card payment can sit as pending for days, so your available balance is lower than the number on screen. Check the available balance, not just the posted balance, before you spend.

4. ATM fees

ATM fees come in two parts. The bank that owns the machine charges a surcharge, and your own bank may charge you for going out of network. Together, the average total cost of an out-of-network ATM withdrawal hit a record $4.86 in 2025, according to Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study.

The fix is boring and it works. Use ATMs in your bank's network, and check the network map in your app before you travel. If you need cash and there is no in-network machine nearby, ask for cash back at a store checkout when you pay with your debit card. Many stores offer it with no fee. If you use cash often, an account that reimburses ATM surcharges can pay for itself, but read the limit, since reimbursements are often capped per month.

5. Minimum balance fees

Some accounts charge a fee when your balance drops below a required floor, even if the account is otherwise free. The floor might be a daily minimum or a monthly average, and the fee schedule will say which one.

The fix is to know your floor and keep a cushion above it. If your floor is $500, treat $600 as your real zero. If your balance swings a lot, an account with no minimum balance requirement removes the problem entirely. Students and new account holders often qualify for accounts with lower or no minimums, so ask before you assume you are stuck.

6. Paper statement fees

Some banks charge a few dollars a month for mailing paper statements. The fix takes about a minute: switch to e-statements in your app or online banking. You still get the same statement, just as a PDF, and the fee stops. Make sure your email address on file is current, so you actually see the statements.

7. Foreign transaction fees

A foreign transaction fee is charged when your card is used for a purchase processed outside your home country, or in a foreign currency. That includes purchases from foreign websites, not just travel. The fee is usually a percentage of the purchase, often around 1% to 3%, and it stacks on top of the exchange rate.

The fix is to use a card with no foreign transaction fee for those purchases, if you have one. If you do not, pay in the local currency when a terminal or website offers a choice. Choosing your home currency at checkout sounds helpful, but it usually means the merchant sets the exchange rate, and it tends to be worse.

8. Excess withdrawal and inactivity fees

Some savings accounts charge an excess withdrawal fee if you go over a monthly withdrawal limit. Check your account agreement for the number, then use your checking account for everyday spending and leave savings alone. If you keep hitting the limit, your money may be sitting in the wrong account.

Inactivity fees work the other way. A bank may charge a fee if an account sits unused for months. The fix is simple: close accounts you no longer use, properly, instead of leaving them open and empty. If you want to keep an account, one small transaction every few months usually counts as activity.

A worked example: a year of avoidable fees

This is an illustration with made-up numbers, using Bankrate's 2025 averages for ATM and overdraft fees where noted. Say one account collects all of these in a year:

Fee How often Cost each Yearly total
Monthly maintenance 12 months $12 $144
Overdraft 3 times $27 $81
Out-of-network ATM 24 times $4.86 $116.64
Paper statement 12 months $3 $36
Foreign transaction $600 in purchases 3% $18
Excess withdrawal 2 times $5 $10
Inactivity (dormant account) 6 months $5 $30
Total $435.64

Check the math: $144 plus $81 is $225. Add $116.64 to get $341.64. Add $36 to get $377.64. Add $18 to get $395.64. Add $10 to get $405.64. Add $30 to get $435.64. None of these fees bought anything. Each one had a fix from the table above.

Ask for a refund

If a fee already hit, call your bank and ask for a one-time refund. This works more often than people expect, especially if the fee is rare for you, the amount is small, or a deposit landed a day late. Keep the call short and polite. Say what happened, say you would like a courtesy refund, and ask what you can change so it does not happen again. You lose nothing by asking, and banks waive first-time fees often enough that the call is worth five minutes.

When it is time to switch accounts

Sometimes the cheapest fix is a different account. Switch when the waiver rules do not fit your life, for example when you cannot keep the minimum balance, or when you keep paying the same fee after fixing your habits. Before you switch, compare the new account's full fee schedule, not just the headline. A no-monthly-fee account can still charge overdraft, ATM, and wire fees. If fees keep coming from cash-flow pressure rather than bad account fit, the deeper fix is a buffer. Frequently asked questions

Can I get a bank fee refunded?

Often, yes, especially for a first-time or rare fee. Call your bank, explain what happened, and ask for a one-time courtesy refund. Be polite and brief. If the fee came from an error, say so and ask for a correction rather than a courtesy waiver.

How do I opt out of overdraft protection in the US?

Contact your bank and say you want to revoke your opt-in for overdraft coverage on ATM and one-time debit card transactions. Ask for written confirmation. After that, a debit purchase that would overdraw your account is usually declined instead of covered for a fee. Checks, recurring debit payments, and ACH payments are not covered by this opt-in rule, so they can still overdraw you.

Do no-fee accounts have a catch?

A no-monthly-fee account simply has no monthly maintenance fee. It can still charge overdraft, ATM, wire, and other fees. Read the full fee schedule before you open any account, and compare the fees you are most likely to trigger, not just the monthly fee.

What is the most expensive bank fee?

For most beginners, overdraft fees cost the most, because one bad week can trigger several of them. The average overdraft fee was $26.77 in 2025, according to Bankrate's 2025 Checking Account and ATM Fee Study. A buffer and low-balance alerts are the cheapest defense.

How do I avoid ATM fees when traveling?

Use your bank's ATM locator before you go, and withdraw cash in larger, fewer trips from in-network machines. If you travel often, an account that reimburses ATM surcharges can help, but check the monthly cap.

Do bank fees affect my credit score?

A fee on its own does not show up on your credit report. But if your account stays negative, the bank closes it, and the balance goes to collections, that can hurt your credit. Fixing fees early is also credit protection.

Your next step

Open your last statement today and circle every fee. Then fix the biggest one using the table above. For most people that means one of three moves: switch to e-statements, check your overdraft opt-in, or set a low-balance alert. Do that one fix today, and set a reminder to check next month's statement.
