Monthly Money Review: A 20-Minute Checklist That Works

A monthly money review you can finish in 20 minutes: a five-step checklist, a worked example with real numbers, and what to do when a month goes badly.

The short version

  • A monthly money review compares what you planned with what really happened, then picks one change for next month.
  • Five steps fit in about 20 minutes: look back, check bills and debts, check your savings, look three months ahead, and pick one action.
  • In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey, 59% of adults had a major unexpected expense in the past year. Looking ahead turns many of those into planned costs.

A monthly money review is one short sitting each month where you check what actually happened to your money, what's about to hit your account, and what you want to change. Most people never do one. In a May 2026 Verasight survey of 1,000 US adults, 28% said they only have a general idea of what they spend, 12.5% just glance at their balance now and then, and 5% don't look at their finances at all. Only about 13% give every dollar a category and track all of it.

You don't need to be in that last group. Once it's a habit, a review takes about 20 minutes, and it catches small problems early: a forgotten subscription, a card balance that keeps creeping up, a bill you didn't know was due. Below you'll find the full checklist, a worked example with numbers, and what to do when the month went badly.

What a Monthly Money Review Is (and Isn't)

Think of your budget as the plan and the review as the reality check. You compare what you meant to do with what you did, then adjust. It isn't a brand-new budget every month, it isn't an hour of staring at bank statements, and it shouldn't feel like a punishment.

How to make a budget when you earn less than $1,000 a month covers building the plan. This post is about checking it.

Pick a fixed date and stick to it. The day after payday works well because the numbers are fresh and you know what you have to work with. The first weekend of the month is another popular choice. The exact day matters less than doing it on the same day every month.

Why Bother? Most of Us Are Guessing

When people start tracking their spending, many find out they were wrong. In a NerdWallet and Harris Poll survey of 2,072 US adults in May 2026, 73% said that realizing they were spending more than they thought had pushed them to track more closely.

The second reason is surprises. The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey found that 59% of adults had at least one major unexpected expense in the previous 12 months. A vehicle repair or replacement was the most common, hitting 30% of adults, followed by a major home or appliance repair (22%) and a big medical bill (21%). The same survey found that 63% of adults could cover a hypothetical $400 emergency using cash, savings or a credit card they'd pay off at the next statement. The other 37% would need another route, like borrowing.

A monthly review won't stop surprises from happening. It does make fewer of them a surprise. You spot the insurance renewal in March when it's due in May, so you can set money aside instead of reaching for a credit card.

One more point from the NerdWallet piece is worth holding on to: thinking about money more without taking a concrete step can just raise your anxiety. That's why the checklist below ends with exactly one action.

What You Need Before You Start

Not much. Have these in front of you:

  • Your bank and credit card statements (or the apps) for the month

  • Your budget, or a rough list of fixed bills if you don't have one yet

  • Balances for any debts and savings accounts

  • A place to write things down, like a notes app or a simple spreadsheet

  • A timer, because it keeps the review from swallowing your whole Sunday

Your first review will take longer, probably 45 to 60 minutes, since you're collecting your bills and accounts into one list. After that, 20 minutes is realistic.

The 20-Minute Monthly Money Review Checklist

Here is the whole routine on one page. Copy it into your notes app and fill it in each month.

Step Time Questions to answer
1. Look back 6 min Did my income arrive as expected? What did I spend in total? Which three categories went over plan? Is there a charge I don't recognize?
2. Check bills and debts 4 min Are all bills paid or scheduled? Are my debt balances going down, flat or up? Which subscriptions renew soon?
3. Check the cushion 4 min Did my savings grow this month? How close is my emergency fund to its target?
4. Look ahead 4 min Which big costs land in the next three months (renewals, fees, trips, gifts)? Does next month's budget need new numbers?
5. Pick one action 2 min What single change would make next month easier? Write it down and give it a date.

How to Do Each Step

1. Look back (6 minutes)

Start with income, since everything else hangs on that number. If you're paid hourly, on commission or as a freelancer, compare it with your lowest recent month rather than your best one. Then add up your total spending and compare each category with your plan. Don't try to explain every dollar. Circle the three biggest gaps and leave the rest.

While you scroll, read the charge descriptions properly at least once. Unfamiliar charges are where forgotten trials and fraud hide. If something looks wrong, contact your bank right away instead of adding it to a to-do list.

2. Check bills and debts (4 minutes)

Confirm every bill is paid or scheduled. Then look at your debt balances. You're not judging them, only noting the direction: down, flat or up. A credit card balance that climbs while your income stays the same is a clear sign your spending is outrunning your plan.

This is also the moment to scan for subscriptions renewing in the next 30 days. Annual ones are easy to miss on a monthly statement. If you can't remember the last time you used something, cancel it now. You can always sign up again.

3. Check the cushion (4 minutes)

Did the transfer to savings actually happen, and did the balance move? Automatic transfers sometimes fail or get skipped when your account runs low. If you're building an emergency fund, note how far you are from your target. How to build an emergency fund on a low income explains how to size it. If you've set money aside for known costs like car insurance or holiday gifts, check that those balances are keeping pace too.

4. Look ahead (4 minutes)

Open your calendar and look three months out. Insurance renewals, school fees, annual memberships, a friend's wedding, a flight home. Write the cost next to each one and divide it by the months you have left. That's what you need to set aside each month. Most people skip this step, and it's the one that stops predictable costs from feeling like emergencies.

5. Pick one action (2 minutes)

Choose one thing, not five. Cancel a subscription, move $25 into savings, call your phone company about your bill, set a weekly limit for eating out, or pay a little extra on your highest-interest card. A small change you actually make beats a big plan you don't. Put a date on it so it doesn't float away.

A Worked Example

Here is how a review looks with real numbers. It's an illustration with made-up figures, not a real person's budget.

Maya takes home $2,400 a month. Her plan gives every dollar a job, including $300 for savings. At the end of the month she lines up the plan against what happened.

Category Planned Actual Difference
Rent $900 $900 $0
Utilities and phone $160 $172 +$12
Groceries $350 $410 +$60
Eating out $120 $215 +$95
Transport $140 $131 -$9
Subscriptions $40 $68 +$28
Fun and shopping $130 $118 -$12
Everything else $260 $236 -$24
Savings $300 $150 -$150
Total $2,400 $2,400 $0

The review takes about ten minutes and shows her three things. Eating out ran $95 over, nearly 80% above plan. Subscriptions cost $28 more than expected, probably because a couple of free trials turned into paid plans. And the overspending came straight out of savings, so she put away $150 instead of $300. Groceries also ran $60 over, but she only notes that for now and checks it again next month, so she isn't changing two things at once.

Maya picks one action: a weekly eating-out limit of about $28, which is her $120 monthly budget spread over the weeks in a month. The subscriptions go on next month's list. It's a modest change, and next month's review will show whether it worked.

What If the Numbers Look Bad?

Some months will. Prices rise, an expense lands, income dips. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey, 16% of adults said they didn't pay all their bills in the prior month, so if that's you, you're far from alone. Work through it in this order:

  1. Cover the essentials first. Housing, utilities, food, transport to work and minimum debt payments come before everything else.

  2. Trim flexible categories before savings. Eating out, shopping and fun are easier to cut than your future, unless your savings are already close to zero.

  3. Call before you miss a payment. If you can't cover a bill, contact the company before it's due. Many will offer a payment plan.

  4. Set next month's numbers to something realistic. A budget you never hit isn't a plan, it's a wish list.

If debt payments are the real problem, a nonprofit credit counseling agency can walk you through your options. Check any agency's credentials before you hand over money or account details.

How Often Should You Review Your Money?

Monthly suits most people because most paychecks and bills run on a monthly cycle. If you track your spending but still overspend, NerdWallet suggests checking in more often. In that case, keep the full review monthly and add a 10-minute weekly look at the flexible categories: groceries, eating out and fun. If everything has run smoothly for a year, some people drop to a quarterly review. Go back to monthly whenever something big changes, like a new job, a move, a baby or a large purchase.

Make It Stick

  • Use the same day and time every month, with a calendar reminder.

  • Pair it with something you like, such as coffee, a playlist or a walk afterwards.

  • Keep a three-number scoreboard in your notes: how much you saved, your total debt, and your emergency fund balance. Seeing the numbers move, even slowly, keeps the habit alive.

  • Write down your one action each month. After six months you'll have six small wins on paper.

  • Use whatever tool you'll actually open. A notes app, a spreadsheet and a paper notebook all work. Our comparison of free budgeting apps can help if you'd rather have an app sort your spending for you.

Monthly Money Review FAQ

How long should a monthly money review take? About 20 minutes once you've done it a few times. The first one usually takes 45 to 60 minutes because you're gathering your accounts and bills.

What should a monthly financial review include? Your income, your spending compared with your plan, bills and debts, savings progress, big expenses coming up, and one action for next month.

Is it better to review weekly or monthly? Monthly for the full review. If you tend to overspend, add a short weekly check on your flexible categories.

What day of the month is best? The day after payday or the first weekend of the month both work. Consistency matters more than the date.

Do I need a budgeting app? No. A notes app, a spreadsheet or a notebook is enough. Apps just save time on sorting transactions.

The Bottom Line

Block 20 minutes on the same day next month, then do your first review this week. It will take longer than 20 minutes. The second one won't.
