Roth IRA vs 401(k) for Beginners in the US

Roth IRA vs 401(k) in plain English: how each is taxed, the 2026 limits, a worked example, and the order most beginners should fund them.

The short version

  • A traditional 401(k) taxes you later. A Roth IRA taxes you now and lets qualified withdrawals come out tax-free.
  • For 2026, you can put up to $24,500 in a 401(k) and up to $7,500 in a Roth IRA, and you can use both.
  • Get the full employer match first, then fund a Roth IRA, then raise your 401(k).

You got your first real job, opened your first paycheck, and now someone at work is asking if you want to join the 401(k). A friend says a Roth IRA is better. Both sound like grown-up money words, and nobody explains the difference in plain English.

Here is the short version: a 401(k) and a Roth IRA are both retirement accounts, but they handle taxes in opposite ways, and that one difference shapes everything else. This guide compares Roth IRA vs 401(k) side by side, with the 2026 IRS limits, a worked example with real numbers, and a simple order for funding them. By the end, you will know which one to pick first and why.

What is a Roth IRA?

A Roth IRA is an individual retirement account you open yourself, at a bank or brokerage, and fund with money you have already paid tax on. Your money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free too. For 2026, you can contribute up to $7,500 a year ($8,600 if you are 50 or older), and your ability to contribute phases out at higher incomes.

A Roth IRA is yours, not your employer's. You choose the provider, you choose the investments from a wide menu, and the account follows you when you change jobs. You can also pull out the money you contributed, not the growth, at any time without tax or penalty, because you already paid tax on it. That flexibility is handy, but it is not a reason to treat a Roth IRA like a savings account. Every dollar you pull out stops growing.

What is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is a retirement plan your employer offers. You contribute straight from your paycheck, and many employers add matching money on top. With a traditional 401(k), your contributions go in before tax, which lowers your taxable income this year. You pay tax later, when you withdraw the money in retirement.

For 2026, you can contribute up to $24,500 of your own pay to a 401(k). That is more than three times the Roth IRA limit. Some employers also offer a Roth 401(k) option, where contributions go in after tax and qualified withdrawals come out tax-free, like a Roth IRA. The trade-off is choice: you pick from the investment menu your employer offers, and the account is tied to your job.

Roth IRA Traditional 401(k)
Who offers it You open it yourself Your employer
Tax timing Pay tax now, tax-free withdrawals later Tax break now, pay tax on withdrawals later

Income limits Yes, phases out at higher incomes No income limit to participate
Employer match No Often, yes

Early access to growth Earnings withdrawals before 59½ may face tax plus a 10% early tax Same 10% early tax may apply

A few notes on that table. The 401(k) limit counts only your own contributions, not your employer's match. The Roth IRA limit of $7,500 is your combined limit across all IRAs, traditional and Roth. And "tax-free" for the Roth IRA applies to qualified withdrawals, which generally means you are at least 59½ and the account has been open at least five years.

The one question that decides it: pay taxes now or later?

Everything in this comparison comes down to one question: do you want to pay tax now or later? If your tax rate is lower now than it will be in retirement, paying now wins. If your rate is higher now, waiting wins.

Most beginners are in the first group. Early in your career, your income, and usually your tax rate, is lower than it will be later. That is why a Roth IRA often makes sense for young savers. But the math is what matters, not the rule of thumb. Here is an illustration with made-up numbers.

Say you have $6,000 of pre-tax pay to put toward retirement, and we ignore growth to keep the math clear.

Case 1: your tax rate is 22% now and 12% in retirement.

  • Traditional 401(k): the full $6,000 goes in. At withdrawal, you pay 12% tax, which is $720. You keep $5,280.
  • Roth IRA: you pay 22% tax first, which is $1,320. The remaining $4,680 goes in and comes out tax-free. You keep $4,680.
  • Winner: the traditional 401(k), by $600, because you paid tax at the lower rate.

Case 2: your tax rate is 12% now and 22% in retirement.

  • Traditional 401(k): the full $6,000 goes in. At withdrawal, you pay 22% tax, which is $1,320. You keep $4,680.
  • Roth IRA: you pay 12% tax first, which is $720. The remaining $5,280 goes in and comes out tax-free. You keep $5,280.
  • Winner: the Roth IRA, by $600, because you paid tax at the lower rate.

Same $6,000, same accounts, opposite results. The tax rate is the whole game. When the rates are equal, the two accounts come out even, before growth. When they differ, you win by paying tax in the year your rate is lower.

What about the employer match?

The employer match changes the order, not the math above. A match is extra money your employer adds when you contribute. This is an illustration with made-up numbers. Say you earn $55,000 and your employer matches 50% of the first 6% of your pay that you contribute. Six percent of $55,000 is $3,300. The match is half of that, or $1,650, added to your account on top of your own $3,300.

That $1,650 is an instant 50% return on your $3,300, before any investment growth. No investment can promise that. That is why the standard advice is to grab the full match before anything else. Skipping it is like turning down part of your pay.

Match formulas differ. Some employers match dollar for dollar on the first few percent. Some match nothing at all. Your plan documents or HR department can tell you the exact formula. Whatever it is, contribute at least enough to get all of it.

Which should you fund first?

Here is the order that works for most beginners in the US:

  1. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match. This is step one, always.
  2. Open a Roth IRA and contribute what you can, up to the $7,500 limit for 2026. This gives you tax-free growth and more investment choice.
  3. If you still have room in your budget, go back and raise your 401(k) contributions. The higher limit lets you shelter more money.
  4. If your employer offers a Roth 401(k), you can use that for step 3 instead, to keep more of your savings in the tax-free bucket.

Before step one, one thing comes first: a small emergency fund. Retirement accounts are hard to tap without penalties, so a surprise bill should not force you to raid them. How to build an emergency fund on a small income walks through building one on a tight budget.

How much should you save in total? A common target is 15% of your pay toward retirement, including the match, once you can afford it. If that sounds steep, start with the match, then raise your contribution by 1% each year or with every raise. Small, steady increases beat a big plan you abandon. How to budget on less than $1,000 a month can help you find the room, and the 50/30/20 rule shows one simple way to split your pay.

What if your job has no 401(k)?

Then the Roth IRA becomes your step one. Open one yourself and contribute regularly. You can also ask whether your employer offers another plan, but do not wait for one. An IRA you fund yourself beats a 401(k) you do not have.

If your income is uneven, the Roth IRA still works, because you can contribute any time during the year and up to the tax filing deadline for that year. How to budget with irregular income covers how to handle retirement saving when pay changes month to month.

What if you earn too much for a Roth IRA?

For 2026, direct Roth IRA contributions phase out for single filers with modified AGI between $153,000 and $168,000, and for married couples filing jointly between $242,000 and $252,000. If your income is above those ranges, you cannot contribute directly. Some high earners use a "backdoor" Roth IRA, contributing to a traditional IRA and then converting it. That move has tax rules and paperwork of its own, so get advice or read the IRS guidance before trying it. For most beginners, the income limits are not an issue yet.

Common mistakes to avoid

Waiting to start. Time matters more than the account you pick. A dollar saved at 25 has decades longer to grow than a dollar saved at 40. Even $50 a month in a Roth IRA beats a perfect plan you start next year. To see why, here is an illustration with made-up numbers and an assumed 7% average annual return, which is not a prediction: $100 a month for 30 years at 7% grows to about $122,000. At 5%, the same deposits reach about $83,000. Past performance does not guarantee future results, but the gap shows why starting early and keeping fees low matters.

Picking investments and forgetting them. A retirement account is a container, not an investment. Once money is in, you still have to choose investments, usually low-cost index funds or a target-date fund that adjusts as you age. A target-date fund is the simplest set-and-forget option in most 401(k) plans. Check what yours holds and what it charges.

Raiding the account early. Early withdrawals from a traditional 401(k) are taxed as income and may face a 10% additional tax before age 59½. Roth IRA earnings have similar rules. Keep retirement money for retirement.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have both a Roth IRA and a 401(k)?

Yes. They have separate limits, so you can fund both in the same year. For 2026, that is up to $24,500 of your own pay into a 401(k) plus up to $7,500 into a Roth IRA.

Is a Roth IRA better than a 401(k)?

Neither is better in general. A 401(k) wins when there is a match or your tax rate is higher now than it will be in retirement. A Roth IRA wins when your tax rate is lower now, or you want more investment choice. Most beginners end up using both.

How much do I need to open a Roth IRA?

Many brokerages let you open one with no minimum, and you can start with $50 a month. You do need earned income to contribute, and your contributions for the year cannot exceed what you earned.

Can I withdraw money from my Roth IRA early?

You can withdraw your contributions at any time, tax-free and penalty-free, because you already paid tax on them. Earnings are different: withdrawing them before 59½ and before the five-year rule is met may mean tax plus a 10% additional tax.

What happens to my 401(k) if I leave my job?

It stays in your old employer's plan unless you move it. You can usually leave it, roll it into an IRA, or roll it into your new employer's plan. Rolling it into an IRA often gives you more investment choice and keeps old accounts from getting forgotten.

Should I pick a Roth 401(k) if my employer offers one?

If your tax rate is low now and you expect it to rise, a Roth 401(k) gives you Roth-style tax-free withdrawals with the 401(k)'s higher limit and match. If your rate is high now, the traditional 401(k)'s upfront tax break may serve you better. Many plans let you split contributions between the two.

Your next step

This week, do two things. First, check whether your employer offers a match and contribute enough to get all of it. Second, if you have room left, open a Roth IRA and set up an automatic monthly contribution, even if it is small. The account choice matters less than the habit. Start, automate it, and let time do the heavy lifting.
