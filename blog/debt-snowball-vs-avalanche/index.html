Debt Snowball vs Avalanche: Which Pays Off Debt Faster?

Debt snowball vs avalanche on the same three debts: months to debt-free, interest paid, what the research says, and how to pick the one you'll finish.

The short version

  • Snowball clears the smallest balance first for a quick win. Avalanche clears the highest interest rate first and costs less.
  • In the worked example the avalanche saves $377 and finishes a month sooner, but the first debt takes 21 months to clear instead of 8.
  • A study of 6,000 people who cleared their debt found that closing accounts, whatever their size, predicted success. The best method is the one you keep paying.

If you owe money in more than one place, the order you pay it off in changes how much interest you pay and how long it takes. With credit card rates where they are, that matters: the average rate on US card accounts that were charged interest was 22.36% in August 2026, according to Federal Reserve data.

The two standard approaches are the debt snowball and the debt avalanche. They follow the same routine and differ in a single decision.

What both methods have in common

  1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment.
  2. Pay the minimum on all of them, every month, no exceptions.
  3. Put every spare dollar on one target debt.
  4. When the target is cleared, take everything you were paying on it and add it to the next one. Your total monthly payment never goes down.

Step 4 is where the names come from. The payment grows as it rolls from debt to debt.

The only difference is how you choose the target.

Debt snowball: smallest balance first

Line up your debts from the smallest balance to the largest and ignore the interest rates. You clear a whole debt quickly, which frees up its minimum payment and proves the plan is working.

Good for: people who have tried before and lost steam, or who have several small debts cluttering the picture.

The cost: you usually pay more interest overall, because an expensive debt can sit untouched while you clear cheaper ones.

Debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

Line up your debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest and ignore the balances. Every extra dollar goes where it is costing you the most.

Good for: people who are motivated by the numbers, or anyone with one debt at a much higher rate than the rest.

The cost: if the highest-rate debt is also a big one, it can be a long wait before anything is fully paid off.

Snowball vs avalanche on the same three debts

Say you have these debts and can afford $286 a month in total. That is the $186 of minimums plus $100 extra.

Debt Balance Interest rate Minimum
Store card $900 15% $30
Car loan $2,000 8% $60
Credit card $3,200 26% $96

Snowball order: store card, car loan, credit card. Avalanche order: credit card, store card, car loan.

Result Snowball Avalanche
Months until debt-free 27 26
Total interest paid $1,591 $1,214
First debt cleared in Month 8 Month 21

The avalanche saves $377 and one month. The snowball hands you a cleared debt in month 8, while the avalanche makes you wait until month 21 for the first one.

For comparison, paying only the $186 with nothing extra takes 60 months and costs about $3,041 in interest. That assumes the payments stay fixed. Real credit card minimums shrink as the balance falls, which stretches things out even longer. Either method beats that by a long way.

These figures use simple monthly interest and are rounded to the nearest dollar. Your lender's exact calculation will differ a little, but the pattern holds.

What the research says

On paper the avalanche always wins. In real life the picture is less tidy.

Researchers at Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Management studied 6,000 people who had eventually cleared their credit card debt through a debt settlement company. They found that people were more likely to get rid of all their debt if they paid off the smallest balances first. Closing an account predicted success no matter how many dollars that account held.

In other words, the feeling of finishing something keeps people going. The maths favours the avalanche. Human behaviour often favours the snowball.

How to choose between them

Pick the avalanche if:

  • one debt has a much higher rate than the others (payday loans and credit cards usually do)
  • you can wait a while for the first win
  • the interest gap in your own numbers is large

Pick the snowball if:

  • you have quit a payoff plan before
  • you have several small balances you could clear within a few months
  • your interest rates are close together, so the avalanche saves little

A sensible hybrid: clear one tiny debt first for momentum, then switch to highest-rate-first for everything else.

Ways to speed up either method

  • Find the extra payment in your budget. Even $25 a month shortens the timeline. A zero-based budget shows where it could come from.
  • Send windfalls straight to the target. Tax refunds, gifts, overtime, money from selling things.
  • Ask for a lower rate. A five-minute call to a card issuer sometimes works and costs nothing to try.
  • Add income for a season. A few months of side income aimed entirely at one debt can wipe it out.
  • Stop adding to the balance. Remove saved cards from shopping sites while you are paying down.

Mistakes that undo the plan

  • Skipping the emergency buffer. With no cushion, the next surprise goes on the card and you are back at the start. Build a starter emergency fund first.
  • Lowering your payment after a debt is cleared. The rolled-over minimum is the engine. Keep the total the same.
  • Ignoring fees and promo deadlines. A 0% offer that expires next month may deserve to jump the queue.
  • Missing a minimum on a debt you aren't targeting. Late fees and penalty rates wipe out your gains fast. Automate the minimums if you can.

Common questions

Does the snowball method hurt my credit score? Neither method does on its own. Paying on time and lowering your balances helps under both.

Should I save or pay off debt first? Keep the minimums going, build a small buffer of around $500, then attack the debt.

What if two debts have the same rate? Pay the smaller one first. You get the win without paying anything extra for it.

The honest answer

A plan you abandon in month five saves nothing, however good it looked on paper. Run your own numbers both ways, look at the gap, and choose the one you believe you'll still be following a year from now.
