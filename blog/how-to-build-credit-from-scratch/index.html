How to Build Credit From Scratch in the US (2026 Guide)

Learn how to build credit from scratch in the US: secured cards, authorized users, credit-builder loans, and a 12-month plan. No hype, just steps.

The short version

  • No credit history is not a bad score. You just need one account that reports to the credit bureaus.
  • A secured card, an authorized user spot, a credit-builder loan or reported rent can each get you started.
  • Pay on time, keep balances low and apply sparingly. A first FICO score can appear after about six months.

If you have never had a credit card or a loan in your name, you probably have no credit score at all. That is normal, and it is fixable. This guide shows you how to build credit from scratch in the United States, step by step, with no hype and no need to go into debt. Everything here follows US rules as they stand in 2026. Credit systems work differently in other countries, so if you live outside the US, check your own country's rules.

What "no credit" actually means

Building credit from scratch means creating a record that shows lenders you repay borrowed money on time. You open one account that reports to the credit bureaus, use it lightly, and pay on time every month. After about six months of that history, scoring models have enough data to give you a first FICO score.

Having no credit history is different from having a bad score. A bad score says you missed payments or ran up debt. No history says the bureaus simply have nothing on you yet. In the US, that is common for young adults. Experian and Oliver Wyman put the 2022 figures at 28 million Americans with no credit file at all, and 21 million more with files too thin to score.

No score is not the same as a score of zero. Credit scores do not go to zero. The most common FICO scores run from 300 to 850. When you have no file, there is just nothing to score yet.

How your first credit score gets made

Three companies, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, keep your credit reports. The two scoring brands you will hear about most are FICO and VantageScore. FICO says its scores are used by 90 percent of top lenders, so this guide focuses on FICO.

FICO weighs five factors for the average person:

Factor Weight
Payment history 35%
Amounts owed 30%
Length of credit history 15%
New credit 10%
Credit mix 10%

Payment history is the biggest piece, so paying on time matters more than anything else. Amounts owed is mostly about credit utilization, which is how much of your credit limit you are using. Length of history is why starting sooner helps, even with a small account.

To get a FICO score at all, your report needs at least one account open for six months or more, and at least one account reported to the bureau in the past six months. One account can meet both rules. VantageScore can appear sooner, as soon as an account shows up on your report.

The 4 ways to start building credit

You only need one of these to start. Pick the one that fits your money and your situation, and do it well for a year before adding more.

Way to start What you need Typical cost Reports to bureaus
Secured credit card A refundable deposit Deposit, often a few hundred dollars Yes, if the issuer reports
Authorized user A family member with good credit Usually free Yes, if the issuer reports authorized users
Credit-builder loan A bank or credit union account Small monthly payments, sometimes fees or interest Yes, if the lender reports
Rent reporting A landlord or reporting service Often free through a landlord, sometimes a service fee Only if the rent is reported

1. Secured credit card

A secured card works like a regular credit card, except you put down a deposit first. The CFPB gives $500 as an example: you deposit $500, and you can spend up to $500 on the card. When you pay the bill, your spending power goes back up. Your deposit is not a payment. It is collateral, and you usually get it back when you close the account in good standing or the card graduates to an unsecured card.

Before you apply, confirm two things: the card reports to all three bureaus, and it has no annual fee or a low one. Use it for one small, planned expense, like gas or a streaming subscription, and pay the full statement balance every month.

2. Authorized user on someone else's card

A parent or other trusted person can add you as an authorized user on their credit card. Their payment history on that card can then show up on your credit report and help you build a file. You may not even need to carry or use the card.

This only works if two conditions hold. First, the card issuer must report authorized users to the credit bureaus. Ask before you count on it. Second, the primary cardholder must pay on time and keep balances low, because their habits become part of your file too.

3. Credit-builder loan

A credit-builder loan flips a normal loan around. The lender holds the loan amount as savings while you make small monthly payments, usually over 6 to 24 months, according to the CFPB. At the end, you receive the full amount you paid. Your payments get reported to the bureaus, which builds your history, and you end up with savings.

This is a good fit if you do not want a credit card, or if a card application was denied. Check the total cost before you sign. Some credit-builder loans charge fees or interest, so a loan that pays out $600 at the end may cost you more than $600 in payments.

4. Rent reporting

Rent is often your biggest monthly payment, and in the US it usually does not show up on your credit report unless someone reports it. The CFPB says positive rental payments can help build your credit, and suggests asking your landlord if they take part in a rental reporting program, often through a rental payment app. Watch for fees these services may charge.

Rent reporting is best treated as a bonus, not your main plan. It may only reach one bureau, not all three, and some scoring models weigh it less than card or loan payments.

A worked example: your first 12 months

This is an illustration with made-up numbers, to show how the math works.

Say you open a secured card with a $300 deposit, so your limit is $300. Each month you charge $45 for gas and pay the full statement balance on time. Your credit utilization is $45 divided by $300, which is 15%. That is under the 30% ceiling many guides mention, and lower is generally better.

In the same month, you open a $600 credit-builder loan for 12 months. Your payment is $600 divided by 12, which is $50 a month. At the end of the year, you get your $600 back, assuming the loan has no fees or interest.

After 12 months, you have 12 on-time payments on the card and 12 on-time payments on the loan, 24 on-time payments in total. Your secured card deposit of $300 is still yours, held as collateral. Your loan payments gave you $600 in savings. You spent $45 a month on gas you would have bought anyway, and you paid no credit card interest because you paid in full.

Here is a simple timeline to follow:

When What to do
Month 1 Pull your free credit reports, pick one starter account, set up autopay for at least the minimum
Months 2 to 5 Pay on time every month, keep card balances low, do not apply for anything else
Month 6 You may now qualify for a first FICO score, since one account has been open six months
Months 7 to 9 Check your reports for errors, keep the same habits, ignore score swings week to week
Month 12 Review the year, ask whether your secured card can graduate, decide if a second account makes sense

The 5 habits that actually move the number

Pay on time, every time. Payment history is 35% of a FICO score, the largest single factor. One payment more than 30 days late can hurt a new file fast. Set up autopay for at least the minimum, and pay the full statement balance when you can.

Keep balances low. Utilization is the part of amounts owed that you control month to month. A card issuer usually reports your statement balance, so the balance on your statement is what the bureaus see. One fix is to pay most of the balance a few days before the statement closes, so a small balance is reported. You do not need to carry a balance to build credit.

Keep your oldest account open. Length of history counts for 15% of a FICO score, and your oldest account sets the tone. Once your first no-annual-fee card is in good standing, leave it open even if you stop using it much.

Apply sparingly. Each application can add a hard inquiry to your report, and several new accounts in a short time can pull your score down. When you are starting out, one well-chosen account is enough. Give it six to twelve months before you think about a second one.

Check your reports. In the US, you can get your credit reports free every week from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com, the only site authorized by federal law for this purpose, according to the FTC. Reports do not include your score, but they show your accounts, balances, and payment history. Checking your own report never hurts your score.

Mistakes that slow you down

Applying for several cards at once. It is tempting to apply everywhere after a denial, hoping one issuer says yes. Each application adds an inquiry, and a stack of new accounts makes a thin file look risky.

Carrying a balance to "build credit faster." This is a myth. Paying interest does not raise your score. What builds your score is on-time payments and low reported balances.

Using products that do not report. The CFPB notes that debit cards, cash, prepaid cards, payday loans, and many buy-here-pay-here auto loans do not build your credit history, because those payments are not reported to the bureaus.

Paying a credit repair company to fix a blank file. There is nothing to repair when you have no history, and no company can remove accurate information from your report. You can dispute errors yourself for free. Save that money for your deposit or your emergency fund instead.

Forgetting the budget behind the card. A credit card is a payment tool, not extra income. The 50/30/20 rule can help you fit a card payment and a loan payment into your month, and the free bill tracker shows how many days are left before each payment is due. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build credit from scratch?

A FICO score needs about six months: one account open for six months or more, with activity reported in the past six months. A VantageScore can appear sooner, once an account shows up on your report.

Can I build credit without a credit card?

Yes. A credit-builder loan builds credit with no card at all, and being an authorized user on someone else's card can add history to your file. Rent reporting can help too if your landlord reports it.

Does checking my own credit hurt my score?

No. Checking your own reports or scores is a soft inquiry, and it does not affect your score. Hard inquiries happen when you apply for new credit, and those can lower your score a little for a short time.

What credit score do I start with?

You do not start with a number. You start with no score at all. Your first score depends on what is in your report: your payment history, your balances, and how long your accounts have been open.

Does paying rent build credit?

Only if the rent is reported. In the US, most landlords do not report rent by default. Ask your landlord if they use a rental reporting program, and check for fees.

Do I need a job to build credit?

You need income to get a credit card in your own name, and if you are under 21 you may need to show proof of income or have a cosigner. You do not need a job to become an authorized user, since the primary cardholder is responsible for that account. Your next step

Pick one path from this guide, and take the first step this week. For most beginners in the US, that means pulling your free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, then opening one secured card or one credit-builder loan. Pay on time, keep balances low, and let the months do their work.

This article is general information for readers in the United States, not personal financial advice. Credit rules differ by country, and lenders set their own approval rules, so check the terms of any product before you apply.
