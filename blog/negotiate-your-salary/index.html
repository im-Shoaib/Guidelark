How to Negotiate Your Salary (Even Your First Job)

How to negotiate your salary, even for your first job: research the range, pick a number, use simple scripts and handle a no. With real numbers.

The short version

  • Most people who ask for more than the first offer get at least some of it.
  • Research the market range, ask for a specific number, and negotiate perks if the salary will not move.
  • A $3,000 higher start, with 3% raises, adds up to about $34,000 over ten years.

Most people accept the first salary they are offered. They worry that asking for more will make them look greedy, or that the offer will vanish. So they say yes, and they carry that first number with them for years, because raises and future offers are usually built on top of it.

Learning how to negotiate your salary changes that. It is a short, polite conversation, and most of the time it works. This guide walks you through it step by step: how to research your worth, what to say word for word, how to handle a low offer, and what to do if the answer is no. You do not need years of experience. You need a plan and a script.

Why negotiating your salary matters

Salary negotiation is the conversation you have with an employer about your pay and benefits before you accept a job, or when your role changes. You make a case for a number based on market data and the value you bring, the employer responds, and you agree on a package. It is a normal part of hiring, not a confrontation.

The numbers back this up. In a Pew Research Center survey of 5,188 employed U.S. adults, published in April 2023, 28% of workers who asked for more than the first offer got exactly what they asked for. Another 38% got more than the original offer, though less than they requested. Only 35% ended up with the first offer. In other words, about two out of three people who asked walked away with more money.

Most people never ask at all. In the same survey, 38% of workers who did not ask said they simply did not feel comfortable asking. Among workers ages 18 to 29, that share was 46%. If you are young and new to this, you are not alone in finding it awkward. The discomfort is normal. The cost of staying quiet is real.

There is a long-term cost too. In research published in 2026 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, Stanford researchers found that MBA graduates who negotiated earned about $6,450 more than classmates who did not. Because raises build on your starting pay, that gap kept compounding: by retirement, the negotiators had earned nearly $800,000 more. Your own numbers will be smaller or larger, but it shows why a small win early can matter so much.

When should you negotiate your salary?

Wait for the written offer. That is the moment your leverage is highest: the employer has picked you and wants you to say yes. Do not accept on the spot, even if the number sounds good. Thank them, ask for the offer in writing, and ask for 24 to 48 hours to review it. That pause is normal. Employers expect it.

If salary comes up before an offer, keep it general. Say:

"I would like to learn a bit more about the role before I land on a number. Could you share the budgeted range for this position?"

You are not dodging the question. You are waiting until you know the full role, the location, and the benefits before you name a number.

Some employers ask what you earned at your last job. In some US states, they are not allowed to ask at all, and in others they can. Either way, your old pay is not the right anchor for a new role. Redirect to the market range:

"I would rather focus on the value of this role. Based on my research, the range for this position is $X to $Y, and I think that is the right place to start."

How do you research what you are worth?

Your ask has to come from data, not a feeling. Pull numbers from at least three sources, and write them down before any conversation about pay.

  • Government data. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes wage data by occupation and area. It is slower to update than salary sites, but it is solid and free.
  • Salary sites. Glassdoor, Payscale, and Salary.com show what people report earning in similar roles. Treat these as a range, not gospel, because the data is self-reported.
  • Job postings. Many US states now require pay ranges in job ads. Collect ranges from live postings for the same title in your city.

From those three sources, build your range. Write down three numbers: your target, the number you will ask for; your walk-away, the lowest you will accept; and your stretch, a slightly higher number you would love to get. Your opening ask should sit between your target and your stretch. That leaves room to meet in the middle without dropping below what you actually want.

How to negotiate your salary: your prep checklist

Do this prep before the offer call. Ten minutes here is worth more than an hour of worrying.

Step What to do
1 Research the market range from three sources
2 Set your target, walk-away, and stretch numbers
3 List three specific reasons you are worth it
4 Practice your script out loud twice
5 Get the offer in writing
6 Decide what else you will ask for if base pay is fixed
7 Set a deadline to respond, within 24 to 48 hours

Practice out loud, not just in your head. Say your script twice, once to yourself and once to a friend if you can. It will feel strange. That is the point. The second time it comes out smoother, and by the real call it sounds like something you have said before.

What to say: word-for-word scripts

You do not need to be slick. You need to be clear, warm, and specific. These scripts are starting points. Fill in your numbers and say them in your own words.

Script 1: Asking for more

Use this after you have the written offer, on a call or video chat.

"Thank you so much for the offer. I am excited about the role and the team. Based on my research for this role in [city], and what I would bring to the position, I was hoping we could get closer to $X. Is there room to talk about that?"

Script 2: Responding to a low offer

If the offer is below your range, name the gap and invite them to close it.

"Thanks for sending this over. I appreciate the offer, and I want to be upfront: the base is a bit below what I was expecting based on the market range for this role. Would you be open to $X? If the base cannot move, I would love to talk about other parts of the package."

Script 3: Following up in writing

After any verbal agreement, confirm it by email the same day.

"Hi [Name], Thanks again for the offer and for talking it through today. To confirm what we discussed: base salary of $X, start date of [date], and a performance review at six months. I am excited to accept on those terms. Please let me know if I missed anything. Best, [Your Name]"

What if they say no?

First, do not panic. A no on base pay is not a no on everything. Many parts of an offer can move even when the salary cannot. Ask about one or two of these, not all of them at once.

  • A signing bonus, a one-time payment when you start. A signing bonus can fund a goal right away. Sinking funds for beginners shows how to set aside money for costs you know are coming.
  • Extra paid time off, even one or two more days.
  • Remote work days, if the role allows them.
  • An earlier performance review, at three or six months, with a raise tied to it.
  • Professional development money for courses or a certification.
  • Your title or your start date.

Whatever you agree on, get it in writing before you accept. A verbal promise about a six-month review is easy to forget. An email that says “performance review at six months with salary revisited” is much harder to lose. If the answer is no to everything and the offer is below your walk-away number, it is okay to decline. A polite no now beats a resentful yes that you regret in three months.

What a $3,000 raise is actually worth

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. Say you get an offer of $55,000 and you negotiate it to $58,000. That is a $3,000 raise, about 5.5% above the offer, or $250 more each month.

Here is where it gets interesting. Raises are usually a percentage of your current pay, so a higher starting salary makes every future raise bigger too. If you get 3% raises each year, that $3,000 gap grows. Over five years, you come out about $15,927 ahead. Over ten years, about $34,392 ahead. A $5,000 raise works out to about $417 more a month, and about $57,320 extra over ten years at the same 3% raises. This assumes you stay in the role and raises stay steady. It is still a useful way to see what one conversation is worth.

Give that extra money a job. An extra $250 a month can go straight into your budget: part to savings, part to debt, part to fun. Mistakes to avoid

Most failed negotiations fail for the same few reasons.

  • Accepting on the spot, before you have seen the full offer in writing.
  • Naming a number before you know the market range.
  • Apologizing for asking, or over-explaining. A short ask is stronger than a long one.
  • Bluffing about other offers you do not have. It is easy to check, and it costs you trust.
  • Making it an ultimatum. “Take it or leave it” turns a conversation into a standoff.
  • Negotiating only base pay and ignoring the rest of the package.
  • Forgetting to get the final agreement in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Can you negotiate salary for your first job?

Yes. Employers expect it, even from new graduates. You do not need competing offers or years of experience. You need market data and a polite, specific ask. If you skip it, you start behind and usually stay behind.

Can an employer take back an offer if you negotiate?

It is rare when the ask is polite, specific, and backed by research. Employers usually build room into their first offer. The risk goes up if you make demands as ultimatums, or ask for far more than the market supports.

How much more should you ask for?

For an entry-level role, a common range is 5% to 10% above the offer, backed by market data. Ask for a specific number, not a vague “more.” If your research shows the market range is higher, say so and show your numbers.

What if they say the salary is fixed?

Ask about the rest of the package: a signing bonus, extra days off, remote days, an early review, or development money. If nothing can move and the offer is below your walk-away number, it is okay to decline politely.

Should you negotiate over email or phone?

Ask on a call or video chat, where tone comes through and you can respond in real time. Then confirm everything by email the same day, so the agreement is in writing.

What if you already accepted the offer?

You can still negotiate at your first review. Keep a list of what you delivered, learn the market range for your role, and ask for a raise with the same structure: thanks, evidence, a specific number, and a written follow-up.

Your next step

Pick one real job, even one you have not applied to yet. Look up the market range from three sources, write down your target and walk-away numbers, and write out your script. That is the whole prep. The next time an offer lands in your inbox, you will not have to figure this out under pressure. You will already be ready.
