25 Ways to Save Money on Groceries Without Coupons

25 ways to save money on groceries without coupons: plan before you shop, compare unit prices, buy smarter staples, and stop throwing food away.

The short version

  • The biggest savings happen before you enter the shop: check what you have, plan a few meals, write a list.
  • Compare unit prices, not shelf prices. The bigger pack isn't always cheaper.
  • Food you throw away is the most expensive food you buy. USDA estimates 30 to 40% of the US food supply is wasted.

Coupons take time, steer you toward branded products, and often aren't offered where you shop. You can cut a grocery bill more reliably by changing how you plan, choose and store food. None of the 25 ideas below needs an app or a loyalty card.

You don't need all of them. Pick three for your next shop and add more once those feel routine.

What a "low" grocery budget looks like

It helps to have a reference point. The US Department of Agriculture publishes a "Thrifty Food Plan", its estimate of the cost of a healthy diet cooked at home on a tight budget. For May 2026 it put that at about $317 a month for a man aged 20 to 50 and about $252 for a woman of the same age.

Prices vary by country and city, so treat those as a yardstick, not a target. If you are spending far above them on food for one person, there is probably room to cut without eating worse.

Before you leave home

1. Look in your cupboards, fridge and freezer first. Build this week's meals around what is already there, starting with anything close to its date.

2. Plan a few dinners, not the whole week. Four or five planned meals is enough structure. Leave room for leftovers.

3. Write a list in the order you walk the shop. Fewer laps means fewer extras in the basket.

4. Set a spending limit before you go. It is easier to be sensible at home than in aisle seven.

5. Eat before you shop. Shopping hungry reliably adds things you didn't plan for.

6. Shop less often. Every extra trip is another chance to pick up extras. One main shop a week beats four small ones.

In the shop

7. Compare unit prices. The price per 100 g, per litre or per item is on the shelf label in many shops. If it isn't, divide price by weight on your phone.

A quick example: a 500 g bag of rice at $2.40 costs $0.48 per 100 g. The 1 kg bag next to it at $5.20 costs $0.52 per 100 g. The bigger bag looks like the deal and isn't. This one habit catches most false bargains.

8. Try the shop's own brand. For basics such as rice, flour, tinned tomatoes, oats and cleaning products, the difference is often only the packaging.

9. Look at the top and bottom shelves. The most expensive options usually sit at eye level.

10. Buy whole instead of prepared. A block of cheese, a whole chicken, a head of lettuce. Grated, portioned and pre-washed versions charge you for a few minutes of work.

11. Buy fruit and vegetables in season. They cost less and taste better. Out of season, frozen is often cheaper than fresh.

12. Use frozen vegetables and fruit. They keep for months and nothing gets thrown out.

13. Check the reduced section with a plan. Marked-down bread and meat are good value if you will cook or freeze them today.

14. Keep a running total. Add it up on your phone as you go, so the checkout isn't a surprise.

15. Pay with cash, or one set amount. A hard limit makes the trade-offs obvious while you are still in the aisle.

What you buy

16. Build meals around cheap staples. Rice, lentils, beans, pasta, oats, potatoes, eggs. These carry a meal for very little.

17. Use less meat, or cheaper cuts. Stretch mince with lentils or beans. Thighs usually cost less than breasts and are harder to overcook.

18. Buy in bulk only what you will finish. A big bag of rice is a saving. A big bag of salad that goes slimy is not.

19. Drink water. Juice, soft drinks and bottled water add up faster than almost anything else in the trolley.

20. Skip single-serve packs. Buy the large tub of yoghurt or bag of nuts and portion it yourself.

At home

This is where a lot of grocery money is lost. USDA estimates that between 30 and 40% of the US food supply goes to waste. At home the sums are simple: if you throw out even a tenth of a $300 monthly shop, that is $30 a month, or $360 a year, in the bin.

21. Store food so it lasts. Bread in the freezer, herbs in a glass of water, onions and potatoes kept apart. A two-minute search on how to store a specific food pays for itself.

22. Cook once, eat twice. Double a recipe and tomorrow's lunch is sorted, which is one less meal bought out.

23. Give leftovers a night. One dinner a week made from whatever needs using up.

24. Freeze what you won't eat in time. Most cooked meals, bread, sliced fruit and even grated cheese freeze well. Label them with the date.

25. Track what you throw away for two weeks. Keep a note on the fridge. The pattern tells you what to stop buying or buy less of.

If you only do three

Start with 1 (check what you have), 3 (write a list) and 7 (compare unit prices). They cost nothing, take a few minutes, and touch every item in the basket.

How much can you really save?

It depends on where you are starting from, so here is an example with the maths shown.

Say you spend $300 a month on groceries. Planning meals, switching a handful of basics to own-brand and wasting less brings that down by 15%. That is $45 a month, or $540 a year, without a single coupon.

Fifteen percent is an illustration, not a promise. Someone who already shops carefully will save less. Someone who shops daily without a list could save more.

Where the savings should go

Money saved on food disappears unless you move it somewhere on purpose. Decide in advance: the gap between your old grocery spend and the new one goes to your emergency fund or toward paying off debt.

Common questions

Is it cheaper to shop weekly or monthly? Weekly suits most people. Monthly shops save trips but tend to waste more fresh food.

Are frozen vegetables as good as fresh? For most cooking, yes. They are frozen soon after picking and you only use what you need.

Is buying in bulk always cheaper? No. Check the unit price, and only buy what you will finish before it goes off.
