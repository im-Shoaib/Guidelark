How to Split Bills With Roommates Fairly (With Template)

The short version

  • Fair does not always mean equal: split by room size when bedrooms differ, or by income when pay differs a lot.
  • List every shared bill first, and agree which ones are shared at all.
  • Write it down in a simple agreement with a review date, and know who the landlord can chase on a joint lease.

Moving in with roommates can cut your housing costs in half, or better. It can also turn into a monthly argument about who owes what. This guide shows you how to split bills with roommates in a way that feels fair, stays simple, and holds up when a bill jumps or someone moves out.

What "fair" means when you share a home

A fair split is one where each person pays in line with what they agreed to, based on the space they use, the services they use, or what they can afford. Fair does not always mean equal.

That last part matters most. Rooms change, jobs change, and bills change. A split that was fair in September can feel unfair by February. If your system has a review date built in, nobody has to start a fight to fix it.

List every bill before you split anything

Most roommate money fights start with a bill nobody remembered. Before you pick a method, sit down together and list every shared cost.

Common shared bills include:

  • Rent
  • Electricity
  • Gas
  • Water and sewer
  • Internet
  • Streaming services everyone uses
  • Renters insurance, if you share a policy
  • Household supplies such as toilet paper, cleaning products, and trash bags
  • Shared groceries, only if you agree to share food

Next, mark each bill as fixed or variable. Rent and internet are usually fixed, so they stay the same each month. Electricity, gas, and water move with the seasons and with usage.

Decide one more thing at this stage: which bills are shared at all. If one roommate never watches the streaming service, that person should not pay for it. A bill is only shared if everyone agreed it is shared.

How should roommates split rent fairly?

Rent is usually the biggest bill, so it deserves the most thought. There are three methods that work for most households.

Method 1: Equal split

Add up the shared bills, divide by the number of roommates, and everyone pays the same amount.

The equal split works best when the bedrooms are close in size, nobody has a private bathroom or balcony that others lack, and incomes are roughly similar. It is simple, fast, and easy to check.

Method 2: Split by room size

With this method, each person pays rent in proportion to the private space they get. Measure each bedroom, add the sizes together, and divide the rent by each room's share of the total.

(Your bedroom size / Total bedroom size) x Total rent = Your rent share

Say the rent is $1,800 and the bedrooms are 150, 120, and 90 square feet. The total is 360 square feet. That gives rent of $750, $600, and $450. Those three numbers add back up to $1,800.

You can adjust for perks. A private bathroom, a balcony, a walk-in closet, or a parking spot is worth something. Many households add a small flat amount, such as $25 to $50 per month, for a private bathroom.

This method is the best fit when the bedrooms are clearly different sizes. It takes a little more math up front, but it settles the most common argument in shared housing: who pays for the big room.

Method 3: Split by income

With an income-based split, each person pays the same percentage of their income toward shared costs. Add up everyone's monthly take-home pay, work out each person's share of that total, and apply those percentages to the shared bills.

Say three roommates take home $3,000, $2,200, and $1,800 per month. The total is $7,000. Their shares are about 43 percent, 31 percent, and 26 percent. On $2,055 of shared costs, that works out to about $881, $646, and $528.

This method fits households where incomes are far apart, for example when one roommate works full time and another studies part time. It keeps the lower earner from being squeezed out of the home.

Split methods compared

Method How it works Best when Watch out for
Equal split Total bills divided by number of roommates Rooms and incomes are similar Feels unfair with unequal rooms
Split by room size Rent follows each bedroom's share of total bedroom space Bedrooms differ clearly in size Needs measuring and agreement on perks
Split by income Each person pays the same share of their take-home pay Incomes differ a lot Requires trust about income figures

Many households mix methods. A common hybrid is rent by room size, utilities split equally, and groceries kept separate.

How do you split utilities with roommates?

Utilities cause more arguments than rent because they change every month. A simple rule that works for most homes: split fixed utilities equally, and split variable utilities equally unless one person clearly uses much more.

Fixed utilities include internet and any flat-rate trash fee. Everyone uses the connection, so an equal split is reasonable. Variable utilities include electricity, gas, and water.

A few situations call for an adjustment. If one roommate works from home all day while the others are out, that person runs the heat or air conditioning longer. If one roommate runs a space heater, a gaming PC, or a second fridge, that shows up on the bill. Agree on a simple adjustment, such as the heavy user paying an extra $10 to $20 on the electric bill.

Expect seasonal swings. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported that the average U.S. residential electricity bill was $144 per month in 2024, with Hawaii and Connecticut above $200 and states such as New Mexico and Utah far below that average (U.S. Energy Information Administration, May 2025). Your bill will depend on your state, your home, and your habits, so plan for a high month instead of being surprised by it.

A worked example: three roommates, one month

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. It shows how a room-size split for rent and an equal split for the rest works in practice.

Monthly bills:

  • Rent: $1,800
  • Electricity: $120
  • Internet: $60
  • Water: $45
  • Household supplies: $30
  • Total shared costs: $2,055

Bedroom sizes: Alex 150, Ben 120, Cara 90 square feet. Total: 360 square feet.

Step 1: Split the rent by room size.

  • Alex: (150 / 360) x $1,800 = $750
  • Ben: (120 / 360) x $1,800 = $600
  • Cara: (90 / 360) x $1,800 = $450
  • Check: $750 + $600 + $450 = $1,800

Step 2: Split the other bills equally. Electricity, internet, water, and supplies add up to $255, which is $85 each.

Step 3: Add each person's shares.

  • Alex: $750 + $85 = $835
  • Ben: $600 + $85 = $685
  • Cara: $450 + $85 = $535
  • Check: $835 + $685 + $535 = $2,055

Everyone can verify the total in under a minute. When the numbers are visible, the argument about fairness mostly goes away.

Put it in a roommate agreement

A roommate agreement is a private written deal between the people sharing the home. It is separate from the lease. It does not change what you owe the landlord, but it records what you owe each other.

A useful agreement covers:

  • Who gets which bedroom, and what each person pays
  • Which bills are shared, and how each one is split
  • Whose name is on each account, and who pays it
  • The due date for each roommate's share, and how it is paid
  • How much notice someone must give before moving out
  • How a replacement roommate is chosen and approved
  • A review date, such as every six months, to revisit the split

Keep a copy each, and store photos of bills and payment confirmations in a shared folder. A shared spreadsheet such as our free bill tracker shows every due date in one place, and our comparison of free budgeting apps covers other tools.

Should you put all the bills in one person's name?

In most shared homes, at least one bill ends up in one person's name. That is normal, but it carries risks worth knowing about. The person whose name is on the account is the one the utility company or landlord will chase if the bill goes unpaid, even if the others were supposed to contribute.

There is a bigger issue with rent. When roommates sign one lease together, most leases make each tenant responsible for the full rent, not just their share. This is called joint and several liability. Nolo, a legal information publisher, explains that a private split between roommates does not bind the landlord, and that if one roommate fails to pay, the landlord can demand the full amount from any signer of the lease.

A few habits lower the risk. Rotate accounts so one person is not carrying every bill. Set a fixed payment date a few days before each due date. Pay by bank transfer or an app so there is a record. And read the lease before you sign it, because the lease decides what you owe the landlord, no matter what you agreed among yourselves. Tenancy rules differ by country and by state, so check the rules where you live.

What happens if a roommate doesn't pay their share?

Start with a conversation, not an accusation. Most missed payments are forgetfulness or a short-term cash problem, and a calm reminder fixes them.

If it happens again, put it in writing. A short message works: the amount, the due date, and the new date you agreed on. Written records protect everyone, including the person who is late, because they remove arguments about what was said.

If the pattern continues, agree on a plan. Options include a written repayment schedule, a temporary change in shares, or a deadline to find a replacement roommate. Do not quietly cover someone else's share for months without saying so.

If a roommate stops paying rent entirely, talk to your landlord early. Landlords would usually rather keep reliable tenants than start over with strangers, and an early conversation gives you more options than silence.

Frequently asked questions

Should roommates split bills equally?

Equal splits work when rooms, perks, and incomes are similar. They are simple and easy to check. When bedrooms differ a lot in size, or one roommate earns much less, a room-size or income-based split usually feels fairer.

How do you split rent when rooms are different sizes?

Divide each bedroom's size by the total bedroom size, then multiply that share by the total rent. Add a small flat amount for perks such as a private bathroom, a balcony, or a parking spot.

How do you split bills when one roommate earns more?

Use an income-based split for shared costs, or use it only for rent while splitting utilities equally. Each person pays the same share of their take-home pay.

What happens if a roommate doesn't pay their share?

Talk to them first, then put the amount and the agreed payment date in writing. If it keeps happening, set a repayment plan or a deadline to find a replacement. If the rent is short, tell your landlord early. On a joint lease, the landlord can pursue any signer for the full rent.

Should you put all bills in one person's name?

Avoid it when you can. Spread accounts across roommates so no one person carries all the risk. Whoever holds an account should keep payment records, and everyone should pay their share a few days before the due date.

How often should you review the split?

Every six months, or whenever something big changes: a new job, a roommate moving out, a rent increase, or a bill that jumps for several months in a row.

The next step

Pick one method tonight, run the numbers for your home, and fill in the template together. The conversation takes about 30 minutes, and it prevents most of the money arguments that ruin shared homes.
